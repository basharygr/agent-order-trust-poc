# Agent accuracy erodes confirmation, not proof

**Verdict: do not pivot on accuracy grounds.** The best frontier agents today get an exact cart right only **30–60% of the time** on multi-constraint purchase benchmarks (Claude Opus 4.6 57.1% on EComAgentBench, GPT-5 32.7% on ShopSimulator), and the widely quoted **92% "retail" score is an order-service benchmark** (cancel, exchange, return) sitting at its own measurement ceiling, not a checkout number. Model-caused errors are falling fast, roughly 10–20 points a year on cart benchmarks, and on that trajectory spec-driven single-item purchases should reach 85–95% exact-cart accuracy by 2028–2029. But a second tier of error sources does not move with the model: under-specified intent (10–33% of today's failures, multi-turn success stuck near 35% even after training), stale merchant price and stock data (the actual cause of OpenAI's Instant Checkout retreat), shoppers rubber-stamping confirmations (97% approval, 13.6% catch rate), and seller-written persuasion. Those set a structural floor in the low single digits per order, and with the team's own 26%-of-order-value loss per mistake, a 2% wrong-order rate still costs 52 basis points of agent GMV, more than the 42 bp card-not-present fraud rate that sustains a multi-billion-dollar fraud-vendor industry. Every historical analogy (EMV, 3DS2, Waymo, e-signature, SEC market-access rules) shows the same shape: as error rates fall, **universal confirmation shrinks to risk-routed exceptions while the evidence layer becomes the regulated, litigated artifact**. The agentic-commerce standards are already encoding exactly that split: networks solved agent *fraud* with tokens and left agent *error* to merchants, who are told to keep signed intent records or face a "coin flip" dispute. So the pitch components erode unevenly: always-on human confirmation and standalone agent detection commoditize within two to three years; merchant rules, risk-routed step-up, and signed dispute evidence persist. The user's Muse claim is numerically right (Day-14 retention ~22%) but causally unsupported; no source ties the churn to inaccuracy, and Muse's funnel problems are site access and habit, not measured wrong orders. The real threats to this product are low agent-order volume at small merchants today and commoditization of the evidence layer by protocols and PSPs tomorrow, not agents becoming too good.

## Agents get the cart right 30–60% of the time; 92% is an order-service number

The single most-cited "retail agent" figure is tau2-bench Retail, where lab-reported pass^1 climbed from **74.0% (GPT-4.1, April 2025) to 91.9% (Claude Opus 4.6, February 2026)**, an error-rate reduction of about 3.2x in ten months ([Frontier Log](https://frontierlog.ai/benchmark/tau2-bench/retail)). That number should not be read as "92% of purchases are correct." The tasks are customer-service operations on existing orders (cancel, modify, exchange, address change) run against a cooperative simulated user with full tool access, and the benchmark's own audit found that **12% of retail conversations contain a critical user-simulator error** ([tau2-bench paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07982)), so the 8% residual at the top of the leaderboard is inside the measurement noise. No lab has published a Retail score for any flagship since February 2026, and Sierra has moved its frontier to τ³-Banking, where the best models score only 40–55% ([taubench.com](http://taubench.com/leaderboard/?benchmark=core)). The order-service domain is saturated; it tells you little about placing an order.

The benchmarks that actually score a cart outcome tell a different story. On EComAgentBench (662 tasks on real Amazon products, June 2026) **Claude Opus 4.6 reached 57.1% and GPT-5.4 47.0%** exact-product-or-full-rubric accuracy ([EComAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17698v2)). ShopSimulator's multi-turn shopper simulation puts **GPT-5 at 32.65% full success, falling to 24.13% when the task is multi-turn and personalized**, with simulator noise accounting for under 8% of failures ([ShopSimulator](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18225v1)). ShoppingBench's best result is GPT-4.1 at 48.2%, with the constraint-heavy "Coupon & Budget" intent at 30.4% for every model tested ([ShoppingBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04266)). Amazon-Bench, run on the live Amazon site, gives GPT-4.1 59.75% success and a **4.75% "harmful failure" rate** (irreversible or undesired actions), with other models between 4% and 9% ([Amazon-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15832v2)). A September 2026 study of eight open-weight 20–35B agents found exact-cart success between 0.30 and 0.57 ([arXiv 2609.16093](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16093)). Where a human bar exists, agents sit at or just above the *average* shopper and below the *expert*: Agent Q scored 50.5% on WebShop against a 50.0% average-human and 59.6% expert baseline ([Agent Q](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07199v1); [WebShop](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.01206)).

The failure anatomy matters more than the headline rate for a merchant, because it says which mistakes land as refunds. Attribute and variant mismatch dominates everywhere it is measured: **81.5% of EComAgentBench's 12,467 failed rubrics are attribute mismatches**, and accuracy collapses from 51.8% to 27.2% as the number of implicit requirements rises from 0–1 to 3+ ([EComAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17698v2)). Constraint and filter violations are second; 57.7% of OpenAI Operator's errors on Online-Mind2Web were filter-and-sort mistakes such as applying the wrong numeric range ([An Illusion of Progress](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01382)). Hasty action is a real minority: for Claude-4-Sonnet, **nearly 80% of "buy now" errors were purchases made without confirming details or after the shopper had rejected the request** ([ShopSimulator](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18225v1)). Outright attribute hallucination runs around 5–6% of trials for current open-weight models, with weaker ones at 24–25% ([arXiv 2609.16093](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16093)). Easy tasks are already solved: Claude Computer Use hit 90.4% on easy Online-Mind2Web tasks, then lost 31.6 points stepping up to medium difficulty ([An Illusion of Progress](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01382)). The residual gap is concentrated in exactly the places a small merchant's catalog is messiest: options, variants, sizes, bundles, and promotions.

Production volume confirms that agent-completed checkout is still a rounding error. DoorDash's CEO said on its Q3 2026 call that third-party agents including Muse had produced **"maybe several hundred orders in total"** against 993 million orders in the quarter ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/30/doordash-said-its-only-received-several-hundred-orders-from-ai-agents/)). Checkout.com's merchant survey puts agent involvement at 3% of transactions ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/newsroom/consumer-demand-for-ai-shopping-is-forming-fast-but-trust-for-agentic-commerce-is-still-catching-up)), and Microsoft Clarity's telemetry across 5.2 million agentic sessions shows checkout events in 25% of them but "order success" absent from the agentic top ten events entirely ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/checkout-appears-in-25-of-ai-agent-sessions-vs-15-for-humans-clarity-finds/)). Agents reach the checkout page far more often than they complete it.

## Model errors shrink 10–20 points a year; structural errors sit on a floor

The model-capability bucket is large and shrinking. Within benchmark lineages the slope is steep: ShoppingBench moved from 36.6% (GPT-4o) to 48.2% (GPT-4.1) in one generation ([ShoppingBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04266)); EComAgentBench-style accuracy went 39.6% (GPT-5) to 47.0% (GPT-5.4) to 57.1% (Opus 4.6) ([EComAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17698v2)); attribute-hallucination rates fell roughly four-fold between open-weight generations ([arXiv 2609.16093](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16093)). Adjacent domains moved faster still: OSWorld-Verified went from 32.6% in January 2025 to 85% by mid-2026, crossing the 72% crowd-human bar ([Steel OSWorld](https://leaderboard.steel.dev/leaderboards/osworld.md)), and WebArena rose from 8.87% in March 2023 to 71.6% in January 2026 against a 78.24% human baseline ([arXiv 2506.23978](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.23978)). Direct prompt injection, the adversarial vector merchants worried about most, is closing: Anthropic reports browser attack success falling from 23.6% (August 2025) to 3.8% on Opus 5 without safeguards and 0–0.3% with classifiers ([Claude in Chrome GA](https://claude.com/blog/claude-in-chrome-generally-available)), and an independent June 2026 reproduction found **0 of 140 multi-step browser attacks succeeded** against Claude Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4 ([arXiv 2606.05233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05233)). Extrapolating 10–20 points per year from a 57% base puts a 90% exact-cart rate on multi-constraint tasks two to three years out; this is an extrapolation across non-identical benchmarks, but the direction and rough magnitude are consistent across every series found.

Two different things are being measured, and they move at different speeds. **Accuracy** here means pass^1: run each task once and average the successes. **Reliability** means whether the same task succeeds every time it is run, measured as pass^k (all k attempts succeed) or as run-to-run consistency. Accuracy is climbing; reliability is barely moving. Princeton HAL's independent leaderboard reports three separate columns, accuracy, reliability and consistency; its *consistency* column rose only from 0.76 (GPT-4 Turbo) to 0.84 (Claude Opus 4.7) while accuracy went from 44% to 79%, and the authors conclude that "overall reliability shows only small improvements over time while accuracy steadily climbs" ([HAL reliability](https://hal.cs.princeton.edu/reliability)). The ICML 2026 paper behind it states that "recent capability gains have only yielded small improvements in reliability" ([arXiv 2602.16666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16666)), and the Stanford AI Index 2026 reports agents "still fail roughly one in three attempts on structured benchmarks" ([AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_2_technical.pdf)). No lab has published a pass^8 consistency figure for any 2025–2026 flagship; the last public datapoint is GPT-4o's drop from 61% pass^1 to under 25% pass^8 in 2024 ([tau-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)). METR's horizon data show the same tail problem from another angle: the frontier 50% horizon is roughly 12–17 hours but the **80% horizon is only about 1.5–3 hours**, and METR has never measured a 99% horizon because its suite cannot support one ([METR time horizons](https://metr.org/time-horizons/); [LEAP](https://leap.forecastingresearch.org/forecasts/80-8-hour-task-horizon-1)). For a merchant the distinction is practical: each order is a single draw, and an agent at 92% accuracy could either fail the same 8% of order types every time (learnable, and merchant rules can target them) or fail any order with 8% probability (not learnable, and only per-order confirmation or evidence helps). The thin pass^k data suggest a mix: GPT-4o's pass^8 was far above what independent draws would predict, so failures are partly task-correlated, but each model also carries a large random component. What a purchase needs is a low per-order failure probability, and no published series tracks either half of that question for a 2025–2026 model.

The second tier of errors does not respond to better models at all, and this is the crux for the product. Under-specified intent accounts for **10% of failures when benchmark authors label them (tau-bench, 4 of 40) and 33% when an independent team re-labels the same benchmark (AgentRx)** ([tau-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045); [AgentRx](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02475)); ShopSimulator found multi-turn clarification success "stays around 35% even after training" ([ShopSimulator](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18225)), and a general study found clarification "rarely restores baseline performance" ([LHAW](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.10525)). Merchant data is a floor the agent inherits: DataFeedWatch's 4.5-million-product sample found **7% of Google Shopping items disapproved for data errors** ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/top-data-errors-that-cause-google-shopping-to-disapprove-products-433993)); Google now warns that products whose price or availability changes more than once a day may simply be disapproved ([Google help](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6098372?hl=en)) and its agentic checkout refuses to complete if the final price exceeds what the shopper confirmed ([Google Pay help](https://support.google.com/googlepay/answer/16421147)); Instacart's found-rate model fell from 93% to 61% when inventory shifted in 2020 ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2020/06/09/instacart-coronavirus-artificial-intelligence)). No agent benchmark even models this bucket; every one treats the catalog as ground truth. Human behaviour is the third floor: Anthropic's 1,053-tester study found **people approve 97% of permission prompts and caught just 13.6% of dangerous commands**, with the catch rate decaying from ~17% early in a session to ~5% after 50 prompts ([Claude Code auto mode](https://claude.com/blog/auto-mode-default-in-claude-code)). And while direct injection is closing, seller-side persuasion is not: Microsoft's Magentic Marketplace found 80–100% of agents accepted the first proposal they received ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/magentic-marketplace-an-open-source-environment-for-studying-agentic-markets/)), and ACES documented position biases that persist across model versions and respond to simple description tweaks ([ACES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630)).

The human comparator shows how low "as accurate as a human" actually is. US online orders are returned at **19.3%** ([NRF](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/consumers-expected-to-return-nearly-850-billion-in-merchandise-in-2025)); fit, size or colour drives about 42% of e-commerce returns ([Narvar via Cahoot](https://www.cahoot.ai/top-return-reasons-and-solutions/)); 21–33% of shoppers have returned an item because it did not match its listing ([Chain Store Age](https://chainstoreage.com/inaccurate-product-information-hurts-online-sales)); and even well-run warehouses treat a 0.5% mispick rate as the threshold of a process problem ([Racklify](https://racklify.com/encyclopedia/what-is-a-mispick-causes-and-operational-impact/)). Putting the tiers together, my estimate (not a sourced projection) is that by 2028–2029 frontier agents will place spec-driven, single-item orders correctly 85–95% of the time and multi-constraint multi-item carts 70–85% of the time, but the per-order rate of a *materially wrong order* at a typical small merchant will floor around 2–5% because intent, data, variant, and rubber-stamp errors are not model errors. This floor is an accuracy estimate, an average across orders; it says nothing about reliability, that is, whether those 2–5% concentrate in predictable order types a merchant can rule on or scatter randomly across all orders. At the team's 26%-of-order-value loss per mistake, **a 5% wrong-order rate costs 1.3% of agent GMV and a 2% rate costs 52 basis points**; for comparison, the industry-average debit card-not-present fraud rate was 41.6 bp in 2023 ([KC Fed](https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/payments-system-research-briefings/card-not-present-fraud-rates-in-the-united-states-after-the-migration-to-chip-cards/)), and that loss line sustains Riskified, Forter, and Signifyd. Agent error at its structural floor is a bigger loss line than CNP fraud.

## Muse's churn is real, but nothing ties it to accuracy; Instant Checkout died on merchant data

The user's Muse figure is approximately right and its interpretation is unsupported. Sensor Tower's September 2026 install cohort shows **Muse Day-14 retention at 22.5%, against 26.8% for ChatGPT**, after a Day-1 rate of 73% that nearly doubled ChatGPT's ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/meta-muse-chatgpt-retention/)); Morgan Stanley put 14-day retention "near 20%," called the gap "unsurprising for an early-stage agentic AI market," and said Muse "needs further product improvements to add use cases and build daily habits" ([Rolling Out](https://rollingout.com/2026/10/19/meta-stock-muse-retention-price-target/)). Meta's own Q3 call claimed 10 million setups, 25% daily use among those who set it up, and improving retention with tenure ([Cardano Capital](https://cardanocapital.substack.com/p/meta-q3-2026-earnings-revenue-beats)). Muse's Day-14 number is four points below ChatGPT's and above Claude's and Grok's, so it is not an outlier; and the drop-off is an app-install cohort metric, not a wrong-order rate. No analyst, journalist, or founder in the record attributes it to inaccuracy. What the record does show is a blocked funnel: Amazon shut Muse out in September 2026 because it "does not identify itself while browsing," and The Register's own test found Muse hit Amazon's anti-bot wall and added nothing to a cart ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/09/21/amazon-shows-metas-muse-ai-shopping-agent-the-door/5297777)); HUMAN Security sees 84% of Muse requests hitting product and search pages ([HUMAN](https://www.humansecurity.com/learn/blog/meta-muse-agentic-traffic/)); and the only merchant-side order count, DoorDash's "several hundred," is tiny. Muse is evidence that agents struggle to *reach* checkout, not that they mis-order once there.

The better production post-mortem is OpenAI's Instant Checkout, and it points at merchant data rather than model reasoning. Launched 29 September 2025 with Etsy and Shopify, it was moved "to Apps" in March 2026 after Walmart found that **purchases completed inside ChatGPT converted at one-third the rate of click-outs to Walmart.com**, with Walmart's Daniel Danker calling the experience "unsatisfying" and "a very temporary moment in time" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/20/open-ai-agentic-shopping-etsy-shopify-walmart-amazon.html)). Forrester's Emily Pfeiffer said the process was "prone to errors," that scraped stock, delivery and shipping-cost data were "inaccurate or out of date," and that only about 30 Shopify merchants were live by February 2026 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/20/open-ai-agentic-shopping-etsy-shopify-walmart-amazon.html)). Amazon's Buy for Me produced the same pattern from the other side: merchants received orders for out-of-stock or discontinued items, found stock photos of products they do not sell, and now cancel every agent order; Amazon's A-to-z Guarantee does not apply and no remedy exists for a wrong variant ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/technology/brands-are-upset-that-buy-for-me-is-featuring-their-products-on-amazon-without-permission/); [Cahoot](https://www.cahoot.ai/amazon-buy-for-me-agentic-commerce/)). Every one of these failures is a merchant-data or transaction-enablement failure that a smarter model cannot fix and that lands on the merchant.

So the user's broader claim, that accuracy rather than standards is the real problem, is half right in a way that matters. Accuracy in the broad sense (wrong-order risk, including stale data) is the binding constraint on adoption today, and consumers say so: **60% of UK adults would stop using an AI shopping agent after one mistake** ([ACI Worldwide](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260628382944/en/Six-in-Ten-UK-Consumers-Would-Stop-Using-an-AI-Shopping-Agent-After-One-Mistake-ACI-Survey-Finds)); 43% want to approve every purchase and only 7% are comfortable with fully autonomous buying ([Riskified](https://www.riskified.com/press/agentic-commerce-pulse-consumer-survey-november-2026/)); 42% worry the agent will buy the wrong thing ([Global Payments](https://investors.globalpayments.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/516/consumers-expect-ai-to-make-15-of-their-purchases-within)). But the consequence of that constraint is that agent-order volume at small merchants is small, not that the trust layer is unneeded. And the question of who pays is wide open: 51–54% of consumers say the AI platform should refund a mistaken purchase ([Riskified](https://www.riskified.com/press/agentic-commerce-pulse-consumer-survey-november-2026/)), **93% of merchants say the AI provider should cover the loss** ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/study_posts/the-agent-advantage-how-merchants-plan-to-win-ai-shopping/)), and only 24% of merchants can even tell when a sale came from an AI platform ([Visa](https://www.visaacceptance.com/content/dam/documents/campaign/shopping-index/2026-global-digital-shopping-index-agentic-edition.pdf)). A merchant who cannot identify agent orders and holds no record of what was authorized cannot make that claim.

## Every analogy shrank confirmation to exceptions and kept the proof

The working hypothesis that liability and evidence erode as standards mature runs against every precedent found, and against the standards themselves. When EMV chips cut counterfeit card fraud by **76% at upgraded merchants** ([TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/cybersecurity/news/252435920/Visa-reports-EMV-chip-cards-thwart-fraud-but-criminals-adapting)), the card-not-present verification industry grew rather than shrank; the Kansas City Fed notes the US avoided the post-EMV CNP spike other countries saw precisely because networks and merchants "had already adopted CNP mitigation tools," and the industry CNP debit fraud rate still rose from 26.1 bp in 2019 to 41.6 bp in 2023 ([KC Fed](https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/payments-system-research-briefings/card-not-present-fraud-rates-in-the-united-states-after-the-migration-to-chip-cards/)). 3DS2 shows what confirmation looks like at maturity: a risk-based challenge that most transactions skip, retained as a liability instrument, and re-expanded when risk rises; Ravelin's 2026 data show frictionless rates declining in 76% of countries while merchants' use of SCA exemptions keeps growing ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/3d-secure-rates-2026)). Confirmation became a dial, never a stage that got removed. Waymo, after a decade of improvement to roughly 19,000 miles per disengagement ([Consumer Watchdog](https://consumerwatchdog.org/accountability/new-ca-dmv-numbers-show-uber-robotaxi-partner-nuro-could-not-go-700-miles-without-human-intervention/)), still staffs about 70 remote-assistance agents for ~3,000 vehicles and has the vehicle call for help "in an ambiguous situation ... even if the ADS can confidently proceed" ([Waymo letter to Sen. Markey](https://assets.ctfassets.net/7ijaobx36mtm/7E5uOzS5F7Z1yuFoz27BIc/680a27f89a3aae48977db655a5f45005/Sen._Markey_RA_Letter_Waymo__Response.pdf)). Where the action became cheap and automated, the record became the product: DocuSign's Certificate of Completion is "the audit trail that would be required in court" ([DocuSign](https://www.docusign.com/blog/are-electronic-signatures-admissible-in-court)), and SEC Rule 15c3-5 pre-trade controls were still being enforced against broker-dealers in 2025 ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2025/33-11351.pdf)). Self-checkout is the counter-case in the other direction: when residual error proved costly, retailers added attendants, item caps, and receipt gates rather than removing the automation ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/walmart-self-checkout-target-dollar-general-costco/)).

The agentic-commerce standards of 2025–2026 have already baked in this split. Worldpay's guidance is explicit: when an agent authenticates and a token is validly issued, existing rules apply and the issuer carries *fraud* risk, but for non-fraud disputes where the "agent misread an instruction, bought the wrong item, or exceeded its authority" **"no clean answer exists yet,"** Reg E has "no framework for an agent that buys the wrong thing," and without a signed mandate an agent-versus-shopper dispute is "a coin flip" that issuers tend to resolve for the cardholder ([Worldpay](https://worldpay.com/en/insights/articles/agentic-commerce-liability-is-still-being-written)). Neither Visa nor Mastercard has published a binding chargeback rule for agent-error disputes ([Banks & Bankers](https://banksandbankers.com/agentic-commerce-liability-gap-fraud-vs-agent-error/)). Google's AP2 describes its mandate chain as "a non-repudiable, cryptographic audit trail for every transaction, aiding in dispute resolution," and lists accountability for an incorrect transaction as an open question across user, developer, merchant, issuer and PSP ([AP2 docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google-agentic-commerce/AP2/main/docs/index.md)). UCP requires that checkout "be finalized manually by the user through a trusted UI unless the AP2 Mandates extension is supported" and gives merchants a `requires_escalation` status to pull a human in ([UCP checkout spec](https://ucp.dev/specification/shopping/checkout/)). Mastercard's Verifiable Intent, in its own words, "allows merchants to assess whether additional confirmation is needed before completing an agentic transaction" ([Mastercard](https://www.mastercard.com/us/en/news-and-trends/stories/2026/verifiable-intent.html)). The loosening path is also pre-built: AP2 lets a shopping agent sign a cart "using its Agent Key instead of getting that approval" in autonomous mode ([AP2 specification](https://ap2-protocol.org/ap2/specification/)), and Anthropic's browser agent now auto-approves actions via a classifier with manual approval as an opt-out ([Claude in Chrome GA](https://claude.com/blog/claude-in-chrome-generally-available)). The gate that is loosening is the *shopper-facing* confirmation inside the agent; what the specs keep constant is the signed record and the merchant's right to step up.

## Verdict: confirmation erodes, rules and evidence persist, volume is the live risk

The four components of the pitch do not share a fate, and the conditions differ for each.

| Pitch component | 2–5 year outlook | What drives it | Condition that would kill it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent-order detection | Commoditizes for compliant agents; persists for non-identifying ones | Visa TAP signs agent identity into HTTP headers ([Visa](https://developer.visa.com/capabilities/trusted-agent-protocol/trusted-agent-protocol-specifications)); Forter says every merchant is "effectively opted in" ([Forter](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/forter-turns-ai-shopping-agent-120000608.html)); Muse does not identify itself ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/09/21/amazon-shows-metas-muse-ai-shopping-agent-the-door/5297777)) | CDN/PSP (Cloudflare, Stripe, Shopify) ships agent identification free by default |
| Merchant rules (caps, step-up, allowlists) | Persists; this is the mature form of confirmation | 3DS2 risk routing; Verifiable Intent constraint types ([verifiableintent.dev](https://verifiableintent.dev/)); 75% of merchants call real-time revocation critical ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/newsroom/consumer-demand-for-ai-shopping-is-forming-fast-but-trust-for-agentic-commerce-is-still-catching-up)) | Platform-side mandates enforce caps upstream so the merchant never needs its own |
| Human confirmation with signed approval | Universal gate erodes; risk-routed gate survives | 97% rubber-stamp rate ([Anthropic](https://claude.com/blog/auto-mode-default-in-claude-code)); platforms already own the in-agent confirmation; break-even arithmetic below | Error rates below ~5% plus a conversion cost above ~5% make always-on confirmation uneconomic |
| Dispute evidence (signed intent, cart hash, approval record) | Persists and becomes the core | "Coin flip" without a mandate ([Worldpay](https://worldpay.com/en/insights/articles/agentic-commerce-liability-is-still-being-written)); no agent-error chargeback rule; pays at any non-zero error rate because it costs no conversion | Platform becomes merchant of record and absorbs agent error, or the PSP stores and surfaces the mandate for free |

The break-even arithmetic explains why always-on confirmation is the component most exposed to accuracy gains. Confirming every order pays when *e × L × r > c × m*, where *e* is the per-order error rate, *L* the loss per wrong order, *r* the share of wrong orders the gate catches, *c* the conversion lost to the extra step, and *m* the margin per order. With the team's *L* = 26% of order value, a 30% contribution margin, *r* = 0.8, and *c* in the 3–8% range vendors attribute to a 3DS challenge ([2accept](https://www.2accept.net/blog/challenge-vs-frictionless-3ds2)), the break-even error rate is roughly **4–12%**; using Stripe's claim that 20% of challenge flows are abandoned ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/payments/authentication)) it rises to about 29%. Today's cart-benchmark failure rates of 40–70% sit far above any of these, so universal confirmation pays now; at the 2–5% structural floor projected for 2028–2029 it stops paying on every order and must be routed to the risky minority, exactly as 3DS2 and Waymo route theirs. Two caveats cut the other way. Walmart's finding that in-chat checkout converted three times worse than a redirect ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/20/open-ai-agentic-shopping-etsy-shopify-walmart-amazon.html)) suggests a merchant-surface confirmation may currently *raise* conversion relative to an opaque in-agent purchase, so *c* could be negative for some time. And rubber-stamping means *r* falls with volume, which argues for rare, high-salience confirmations rather than more of them.

The evidence component has *c* ≈ 0, so its economics are set by dispute frequency alone, and the dispute regime is moving toward, not away from, merchants. OpenAI's retreat pushed checkout back onto merchant surfaces ([Retail Dive](https://retaildive.com/news/walmart-sparky-chatgpt-instant-checkout/815647/)); Perplexity's Instant Buy makes the retailer the merchant of record ([secnews.gr](https://www.secnews.gr/en/725171/paypal-perplexity-instant-buy-agentic-commerce-2026/)); Amazon's Buy for Me leaves returns and service with the brand and excludes its own guarantee ([Cahoot](https://www.cahoot.ai/amazon-buy-for-me-agentic-commerce/)); Amex's Agent Purchase Protection covers the cardholder, not the merchant ([Worldpay](https://worldpay.com/en/insights/articles/agentic-commerce-liability-is-still-being-written)). Every live arrangement puts agent-error loss on the merchant by default and gives the merchant nothing but its own records to contest it. The one scenario that would genuinely erase this component is a platform taking merchant-of-record status and absorbing agent error, which is what 93% of merchants and half of consumers say they want; but even then the merchant needs proof to claim against the platform, so the need shifts from defence to recovery rather than disappearing.

Two risks loom larger than agent competence. The first is volume: agents complete a tiny share of orders today, forecasts of 10–25% of US online retail by 2030 are gated by consumer trust rather than any accuracy threshold, and Bain expects specification-driven categories to move first ([Digital Commerce 360](https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2025/12/22/bain-agentic-ai-us-ecommerce-sales-2030/)). A small merchant with a handful of agent orders a month will not pay for a trust layer regardless of error rates. The second is commoditization: the standards encode the evidence layer, and Shopify stores are already auto-enrolled in Google and Microsoft agent checkouts ([xgentech](https://xgentech.net/blogs/resources/shopify-google-ai-mode-gemini-checkout-opt-out); [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/01/08/microsoft-ai-copilot-checkout)), so the platform or PSP could ship mandate storage and dispute packets to every merchant for free. The defensible position is the part of the stack the protocols leave to the merchant: deciding *which* orders warrant step-up, enforcing merchant-specific rules the mandate does not know about (variant-level availability, bundle logic, regional restrictions), and assembling the merchant-side half of the dispute record, including the one error source the merchant actually controls, its own price and stock data.

## Conclusion

The two hypotheses in play resolve into one picture once accuracy is split into its model and structural halves. The user is right that accuracy, broadly defined, is what holds agentic checkout back today, and that is precisely why the product's market is small now; the team's old hypothesis that standards erode the evidence layer is backwards, because the standards are where the evidence requirement is being written down. What neither framing anticipated is that the merchant-side floor on wrong orders is set by data, intent, and human attention rather than model reasoning, which means better models convert the pitch from "catch the agent's mistakes" into "route the risky few and keep the proof," without ever removing it. That is the same conversion EMV, 3DS2, and e-signature went through, and in each case the proof layer ended up as the compliance norm that outlived the error rate that justified it.

The practical implication is a re-sequencing rather than a pivot. Build the signed-intent and dispute-evidence record as the durable core, make confirmation risk-routed from the first version so it does not depend on high error rates to pay for itself, treat agent detection as a short-lived wedge that protocols will absorb, and track two numbers no one has published: the real wrong-order rate on agent-placed orders at small merchants, and the monthly count of such orders. The first will tell you when to tighten the routing threshold; the second will tell you whether the market has arrived at all.
