# Agent orders multiply, yet nobody publishes error rates

Across every major shopping agent, the only directly observed count of agent-completed orders in the May–3 November 2026 window is DoorDash's **"maybe several hundred"** from Muse, Instinct and Fo combined, against 993 million DoorDash orders in the same quarter; every other volume figure is an engagement metric (Meta's 10M+ Muse set-ups, Amazon's 400M+ Alexa for Shopping users, Shopify's 3x AI-referred orders), a growth multiple off an undisclosed base (Forter's 7.2x since August, Stripe's unverified 38x), or a single agency panel's estimate that agents completed **1.52% of US e-commerce orders in October 2026** (FirstPageSage, low confidence). Visa called agentic volume "a very small amount," Mastercard said "the numbers are not yet significant," and Amazon's Andy Jassy said "few customers are doing much shopping this way yet." Not one operator, card network, processor or fraud vendor has published an order-accuracy, wrong-item, cancellation, return or dispute rate for agent orders; the only production outcome data anywhere is Forter's network cancellation split (customer-initiated 1–2%, Google 10%; merchant-initiated 27–37% on scraping-agent orders outside the US), which Forter attributes mostly to agents ordering outside supported geographies, and Forter is holding its fraud and dispute figures for a 12 November webinar. Autonomy moved in both directions: OpenAI removed ChatGPT agent mode and shut the Atlas browser in July–August without a stated reason, Amazon walled off Muse and other unidentified agents, and Walmart, Google, Meta, Anthropic and Shopify all shipped approval-by-default designs with spend caps or hard price ceilings, while Amazon alone added unattended Auto-Buy. For a founding team weighing a merchant-side trust layer, the data say the binding constraint today is order volume and site access rather than measured wrong orders, and the case for detection, rules, confirmation and dispute evidence rests on consumer fear (58% worry an agent will buy the wrong item), merchant liability expectations (93% want the AI provider to pay) and the conspicuous blank where the outcome rates should be. Shopify's Q3 release (3 Nov), Uber, Wayfair and Mercado Libre (4 Nov), Forter's webinar (12 Nov) and Walmart's Q3 FY27 (mid/late Nov) are the next chances for that blank to fill.

## Agent-completed orders: hundreds at DoorDash, about 1% of US orders at the most generous estimate

The single hard number in the window came from an earnings call, not from any agent operator. On DoorDash's Q3 call on 29 October, analysts framed the total from third-party agents as "maybe several hundred orders in total" across Muse, Instinct and Fo, and CEO Tony Xu did not dispute it, calling it "a very low use case" and explaining that "people come to DoorDash and they come back to DoorDash. It's easy to use" ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/29/doordash-dash-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/30/doordash-said-its-only-received-several-hundred-orders-from-ai-agents/)). Set against **993 million quarterly orders**, that is three to four orders of magnitude below first-party volume ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/10/30/out-of-993-million-doordash-orders-ai-agents-only-placed-a-few-hundred/)). Xu's explanation is habit and ease, not accuracy, and that framing recurs across the window: Amazon's Jassy told Q3 investors that external horizontal agents are not "meaningfully impacting Amazon Ads" and that "few customers are doing much shopping this way yet" because it is not yet more convenient than shopping with the retailer directly ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/30/amazon-amzn-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)).

The payments networks, which see every card transaction, confirmed the scale without quantifying it. Visa's Ryan McInerney reported "nearly 50 AI partners live and in production" on its Q4 FY2026 call on 27 October but described agentic transactions as "albeit limited," "still very early" and "a very small amount" of the business, and said they "remain very small" for FY2027 ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/28/visa-v-q4-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)). Mastercard's Michael Miebach said two days later that "agentic transactions are ramping up at impressive weekly growth rates" but "the numbers are not yet significant in the grand scheme" ([AOL transcript](https://www.aol.com/articles/mastercard-ma-q3-2026-earnings-191318000.html)). Neither network gave a count, a dollar volume, a merchant count or a dispute statistic, and an aggregator tracking the space states outright that no network, issuer or processor has disclosed transaction counts for agent-initiated payments ([Axis Intelligence](https://axis-intelligence.com/agentic-payments-statistics/)). Stripe's John Collison said on a 12 October podcast that agentic commerce "only as of September basically started going vertical" without showing numbers ([Forkast](https://forkast.news/?p=132104)); the widely repeated claim that agent-driven Link transactions rose 38x month over month in September, with ~200 connected agents, appears only in secondary reporting with no Stripe primary and should be treated as unverified ([Winzheng](https://www.winzheng.com/en/article/stripe-ai-agent-38x-payment-surge-september-2026)).

The growth multiples that do exist come from one fraud vendor's network and all share the same cause. Forter's post updated 2 November, covering 23 July to 29 October, reports agentic orders **up 7.2x since the first week of August** and 41% week over week since Muse's launch, with Muse placing 7.3x as many orders in its first 39 days as Google's checkout did in 13 weeks, and OpenAI, Google and ChatGPT Agent orders combined growing only 4% month over month ([Forter](https://www.forter.com/blog/ai-agent-order-data/)). Forter does not publish its base count or its network's share of e-commerce, so 7.2x is a slope without an intercept. FirstPageSage, an SEO agency, is the only source attempting an absolute share: its panel of 5,212 US shoppers, 46 brands and 19,850 prompts, normalized to Census e-commerce totals, puts agent-completed orders at 0.19% of US e-commerce in Q1 2026, 0.68% in Q3, **1.35% in September and 1.52% in October**, with Muse accounting for 71% of the Q3 increase ([FirstPageSage](https://firstpagesage.com/seo-blog/agentic-commerce-statistics)). The methodology is opaque, the figures were revised between fetches of the same page, and no peer source corroborates them; the number is best read as an upper bound from a motivated publisher. Independent network measurement is consistent with that ceiling: Cloudflare Radar's share of AI-bot requests declared as "user action" (fetch on behalf of a user) ran 2.45% in Q2 2026, 4.80% in August and 3.11% in September ([TechnologyChecker compilation of Radar](https://technologychecker.io/blog/ai-adaption-trends-data-insights)), and HUMAN Security's June 2026 edition put checkout and payment flows at 2.34% of agent activity ([HUMAN](https://www.humansecurity.com/learn/blog/state-of-agentic-traffic-june-2026-browser-agent-tooling-for-developers-is-catching-on-fast/)). Every independent lens lands in the same place: agent-completed commerce is a low-single-digit-percent phenomenon at most, and the September–October inflection is one new entrant.

Microsoft Clarity's 5.2-million-session study, published 29 October and covering 13 August to 9 September, is the largest cross-site telemetry on agent behavior and points the wrong way for completion. Checkout smart-events appeared in **25.21% of agentic sessions versus 14.65% of human sessions**, but add-to-cart was lower for agents (2.33% vs 3.31%) and "order success," which sits in the human top ten at 1.79%, does not appear in the agentic top ten at all; Microsoft itself warns that sessions may include human handoff and that events "should not be interpreted as steps in a single conversion funnel or proof that an agent independently completed a task" ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/checkout-appears-in-25-of-ai-agent-sessions-vs-15-for-humans-clarity-finds/)). PPC Land also found that dividing the published 306,621 checkout sessions by 25.21% implies a base of about 1.22 million sessions rather than 5.2 million, and that at least 78% of agentic sessions recorded none of the ten most common actions ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/checkout-appears-in-25-of-ai-agent-sessions-vs-15-for-humans-clarity-finds/)). The pattern of agents reaching checkout pages far more often than they complete them is consistent with the earlier trajectory report's reading of Muse as a blocked funnel, and the new window adds the direct cause: a Billions Network audit of 51 large US retailers found 5 explicitly restrict agents, 37 block them by accident through legacy bot rules, and none offers a verified path for an agent to complete a purchase ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/01/jpmorgan-amazon-ai-agents-meta-muse.html)).

Consumer surveys measure the same gap from the demand side. The share who say they have actually completed a purchase inside an AI tool clusters at **11%** in Riskified's five-market September wave of 5,100 consumers ([Riskified via Agentic Commerce Frontier](https://agentcommerce.substack.com/p/the-agentic-commerce-frontier-october-a4f)), 11% in NMI's August survey of 1,000 US adults ([NMI](https://www.nmi.com/about-us/news/nmi-research-consumers-want-ai-to-help-them-shop-not-control-their-spending/)) and 8% of all shoppers in Product.ai's US sample ([Product.ai](https://product.ai/research/trust-in-ai-commerce-report/)), essentially unchanged from Riskified's 13% in October 2025 ([CX Dive](https://www.customerexperiencedive.com/news/consumers-ai-research-few-purchase/803156/)) even as comfort metrics moved sharply (the share who would trust no company with AI-led shopping fell from 47% to 17% between Riskified's Q2 and Q3 waves). Willingness to let an agent buy unsupervised sits in a stable 7–16% band: 7% in Riskified, 7% in ACI Worldwide's fashion wave ([Business Wire via Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/392490/only-7-of-fashion-shoppers-trust-ai-to-buy-for-them-today-showing-merchants-must-build-confidence-in-agentic-commerce)), 9% in Accenture's 16-country pulse ([AI News](https://www.artificialintelligence-news.com/news/ai-shopping-agents-consumer-trust-accenture-report/)), 10% in NMI and 16% in Deloitte's 4,000-person holiday survey ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/commerce/consumers-arent-ready-to-let-ai-do-their-holiday-shopping-yet/)). Self-reported "completed a purchase via AI" at 8–13% is irreconcilable with DoorDash's several hundred orders and FirstPageSage's 1.5%, which means respondents are counting AI-assisted research followed by a human checkout; the behavioral number for autonomous purchase is far lower than any survey shows.

## Accuracy in production: Forter's cancellation split is the only outcome number, and it measures geography more than wrong items

No operator, retailer, network, processor or fraud vendor published an order-accuracy, wrong-item, cancellation, return or dispute rate for agent orders between May and 3 November 2026. That sentence holds for OpenAI, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, Perplexity, Walmart, Shopify, Instacart, DoorDash, Uber, Klarna, Instinct, Fo, Anthropic, Alibaba, Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com, PayPal, Worldpay, Signifyd, Riskified, Chargeflow, Sift, Kount and Ravelin, and a Q4 tracker states it plainly: "No order counts or conversion rates are published for Universal Cart, Google checkout, Buy for Me or Alexa for Shopping" ([Sanbi](https://sanbi.ai/blog/agentic-commerce-tracker-ai-checkout)). The earlier trajectory report projected a structural wrong-order floor of 2–5% per order at a typical small merchant once model errors fall; nothing in the production record can confirm or refute that number, because nobody measures the thing it describes.

What exists instead is Forter's network breakdown, and it needs careful reading. Issuer authorization is not the problem: protocol agents (OpenAI Instant Checkout and Google) authorize at **100%**, scraping agents (Muse, Instinct, Grok Bot, ChatGPT Agent) at 98.8%, humans at 98.9% ([Forter](https://www.forter.com/blog/ai-agent-order-data/)). Merchant-side risk approval was the problem: scraping agents' average approval rate was **44% before Forter's 24 September rule adjustments and 73% after**, with Grok Bot gaining 50 points and no material change for humans or protocol agents ([Forter](https://www.forter.com/blog/ai-agent-order-data/)). In other words, the first production effect of agent orders on a fraud stack was false positives, with more than half of legitimate agent orders declined by rules tuned for bots, and the fix was loosening rules rather than tightening them. Customer-initiated cancellations run 1–2% for most agents but **10% for Google**, which Forter pairs with Google's single-item-only checkout and 56%-below-average basket; merchant-initiated cancellations are highest for ChatGPT Agent and Instinct, around 10% for human orders, and reach **27% in Asia/Middle East and 37% in Europe** for scraping-agent orders, which Forter attributes largely to agents ordering from merchants outside their supported geographies rather than to wrong items ([Forter](https://www.forter.com/blog/ai-agent-order-data/)). Agentic travel orders cancel at a rate 10 points above human travel. Forter withholds fraud rates, dispute rates and its total network size, and promises to address "whether disputes are higher" in its 12 November webinar.

The ex-US merchant cancellation figures are the closest thing to a measured "unfulfillable order" rate in production, and they are an order of magnitude above the earlier report's projected floor, but the two are not comparable: Forter's number is dominated by eligibility mismatch (wrong country, wrong currency, unsupported merchant), which is a transaction-enablement failure the merchant's own rules can catch deterministically, not a reasoning error. That distinction matters for product design because it says the first wave of "wrong" agent orders a merchant sees will be orders that should never have been placed at all, flagged by shipping region, payment geography and catalog eligibility, rather than orders for the wrong variant. It also matches the pre-window Buy for Me complaints, where orders arrived for out-of-stock and deleted products with anonymized customer emails ([Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/amazon-buy-for-me-small-business-backlash/)), and the March Instant Checkout post-mortem, where stale stock, delivery and shipping-cost data drove the "prone to errors" verdict ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/20/open-ai-agentic-shopping-etsy-shopify-walmart-amazon.html)). The earlier report's thesis that merchant data, not model reasoning, is the binding error source is consistent with every production datapoint in this window.

Journalist tests in the window reinforce that failures happen before the order, not after. CNN's September Muse test was blocked at Amazon, forced into manual checkout at Target, stalled on a professional-license gate for shampoo and recommended two venues closed for years ([CNN via Superpower Daily](https://superpowerdaily.com/posts/cnn-tries-meta-s-muse-agent-and-finds-useful-plans-but-stalled-purchases)); NBC found several large retailers blocking Muse outright ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/ai-agent-muse-shop-e-commerce-amazon-wal-mart-wayfair-buy-rcna599069)); a Substack tester's Patagonia purchase fell through after Muse admitted "showing the wrong prices" ([AI Ad Economy](https://aiadeconomy.substack.com/p/i-tested-muse-metas-agentic-commerce)); and Business Insider's lunch-order test on 22 October ended with Muse offering a card that turned out to be HSA-linked at payment selection, with no order submitted and no charge ([Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.co.uk/meta-muse-hsa-card-ai-shopping-test-1809348)). Each is an incident of a near-miss or a stall; none is a completed wrong order, and no structured right-versus-wrong shopping-list test of a production agent by a major outlet was found. The closest to a controlled study is Wharton's August report, which ran ~26,000 simulated purchases across six frontier models and found a single external source could shift product choice by up to 99 percentage points and a one-sentence injected "memory" redirected Claude Opus 4.8 away from an objectively superior product in ~75% of runs, but it reports no accuracy or error rate ([Wharton GAIL](https://gail.wharton.upenn.edu/research-and-insights/technical-report-agentic-shopping/)). Product.ai's finding that four assistants gave conflicting or incorrect answers to 220 shopping questions 86% of the time, with wrong prices off by a median of $300, concerns Q&A rather than purchases ([Tech.co](https://tech.co/news/ai-shopping-assistants-not-ready-holidays)). A 600-trial, 48%-failure study that circulates on forums could not be traced to any author or venue and should not be cited ([Gnoppix forum](https://forum.gnoppix.org/t/ai-shopping-agents-arent-ready-to-buy-on-your-behalf-study-finds/7194)).

The outcome evidence that does exist concerns human-completed purchases on AI advice. Blue Yonder's November returns survey found **46% of US shoppers who bought an item on an AI recommendation returned at least one such item in the past year**, with "different than described" and wrong size or fit the top reasons and only 20% trusting AI to recommend the right size ([Retail Customer Experience](https://www.retailcustomerexperience.com/news/ai-data-issue-driving-product-returns/)). Adobe's September data cut the other way at the traffic level: AI-referred visitors converted at a 90% higher rate and generated 83% more revenue per visit than non-AI visitors, up from 9% worse in August 2025 ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/ai-traffic-drives-more-revenue-per-visit-adobe-499713)), and 69% of Adobe's surveyed AI shoppers said they were less likely to return items ([Digital Commerce 360](https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2026/09/29/adobe-projection-2026-online-holiday-sales-275-billion/)). Both measure humans clicking out of a chat after research, which is exactly the model OpenAI retreated to in March; neither says anything about an agent that submits the order. Fear is measured where experience is not: **58% of NMI's respondents worry an agent could buy the wrong item, size, color or brand** ([NMI](https://www.nmi.com/about-us/news/nmi-research-consumers-want-ai-to-help-them-shop-not-control-their-spending/)), 42% in Global Payments' eight-country sample worry about wrong purchasing decisions ([Business Wire via Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/394701/consumers-expect-ai-to-make-15-of-their-purchases-within-five-years-global-payments-research-finds)), and **60% of UK adults would stop using an agent after one mistake** ([ACI Worldwide](https://investor.aciworldwide.com/news-releases/news-release-details/six-ten-uk-consumers-would-stop-using-ai-shopping-agent-after)). No survey asks whether the respondent has actually experienced a wrong agent purchase. On liability, the alignment is striking and unresolved: 62% of NMI's consumers, 54% of ACI's and 50.8% of Riskified's Q1 wave would hold the AI provider responsible for a mistaken purchase ([NMI](https://www.nmi.com/about-us/news/nmi-research-consumers-want-ai-to-help-them-shop-not-control-their-spending/); [ACI Worldwide](https://investor.aciworldwide.com/news-releases/news-release-details/six-ten-uk-consumers-would-stop-using-ai-shopping-agent-after); [Riskified](https://www.riskified.com/press/riskified-agentic-commerce-pulse/)), and 93% of merchants in the PYMNTS/Visa merchant edition say the same ([Forkast](https://forkast.news/the-agentic-commerce-liability-gap-who-pays-when-the-agent-buys-wrong/)), yet every live arrangement (Google, Perplexity, Amazon Buy for Me, Muse via Shop Pay) leaves the merchant as seller of record and routes wrong items, cancellations and returns to the merchant ([Google Pay Help](https://support.google.com/googlepay/answer/16421147?hl=en-GB)).

## Autonomy moved both ways: OpenAI pulled it, Amazon walled it off, Walmart and Meta capped it

The clearest subtraction of autonomy in the window came from OpenAI, and it came without explanation. ChatGPT agent mode disappeared for users between roughly 8 July and early August, with no deprecation notice, migration guide or blog post; an OpenAI developer-community thread titled "Agent Mode was removed with no real replacement" drew a support reply on 7 September apologizing for the disruption and pointing to a cloud browser inside ChatGPT Work that "can pause for sign-in or user takeover" ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/agent-mode-was-removed-with-no-real-replacement/1389601)). Atlas, the browser whose agent mode had been hijacked into an unauthorized Amazon purchase in Zenity's February demonstration ([Zenity Labs](https://labs.zenity.io/post/grand-theft-atlas)), was announced for retirement on 9 July and stopped working on 9 August, with browser automation for logged-in sites moved to Codex and "agent-led browsing" folded into the ChatGPT desktop app as a productivity feature ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/computing/chatgpt-atlas-is-shutting-down-and-it-has-some-homework-left-before-you-migrate/)). Combined with the March move of Instant Checkout "to apps" after Walmart's one-third conversion finding and CNBC's report that item information "was often inaccurate" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/24/openai-revamps-shopping-experience-in-chatgpt-after-instant-checkout.html)), OpenAI exited every surface on which it completed a consumer purchase within five months, and its October disclosures are entirely about advertising: 100,000+ advertisers, 89 countries, a visual ad format and conversion-measurement partnerships ([Retailgentic](https://www.retailgentic.com/p/october-agentic-commerce-news-roundup)). OpenAI never linked the removals to accuracy or safety; the connection to its earlier statement that prompt injection "may never be solved" for browser agents is inferential. Its documentation remained inconsistent in October, with the merchant page describing purchases on merchant-owned surfaces while developer docs still said Instant Checkout "is available to approved partners" ([OpenAI commerce docs via search](https://developers.openai.com/commerce)).

Amazon moved the other way on its own agent and shut the door on everyone else's. On 20 September it blocked Meta's Muse with a popup citing "an unauthorized AI agent" that "does not identify itself," after asking Meta to remove Amazon from Muse first ([Retail Gazette](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2026/09/amazon-blocks-metas-new-ai-shopping-agent-from-its-website/)); newsletter and security reporting say ChatGPT, Claude, Google and Perplexity agents are also blocked ([Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/security/amazon-blocks-ai-agents-chatgpt-claude-muse/); [Shopifreaks](https://www.shopifreaks.com/amazon-ceo-andy-jassy-says-alexa-for-shopping-will-be-the-main-agent-for-shopping-amazon-even-as-it-blocks-rival-ai-agents/)), though the Ninth Circuit vacated Amazon's CFAA injunction against Perplexity's Comet on 4 August on the reasoning that the human user, not Perplexity, accesses Amazon's servers ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2230471/perplexity-has-successfully-overturned-amazon-injunction-on-its-ai-shopping-bot/)). JPMorgan, Charles Schwab, Neiman Marcus and Kohl's have also moved to block personal agents ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/01/jpmorgan-amazon-ai-agents-meta-muse.html)). Inside its own walls, Amazon is the only operator in the window to add unattended purchasing: Alexa for Shopping's Auto-Buy completes a purchase automatically when an item hits a target price and can stay active for up to six months, while the 100+ actions added on 29 October require review before confirming for purchase-related requests ([TechJournal](https://techjournal.org/alexa-shopping-100-actions)). Jassy's stated reason is strategic rather than technical: "we believe Alexa for Shopping is going to be the primary shopping agent customers use to shop Amazon" ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/30/amazon-amzn-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)).

Everyone else launched or re-launched with gates, and the designs converge. Walmart's Agent Profiles, announced 21 October for launch "within a month," give each third-party agent a dedicated profile inside the customer's account where **approval is the default** or the customer sets a cap such as $50, $100 or $200 within which the agent buys autonomously; Walmart may still require extra approvals within the limit, agents never receive card data, and Walmart's returns process applies "including when an agent doesn't act as intended" ([Digital Commerce 360](https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2026/10/23/walmart-unveils-ai-agent-shopping-controls-spending-limits/)). Google's agentic checkout completes only "if the final price is the same as or lower than the total price" the user confirmed, fails without charge on any price increase or stock-out, lets merchants set minimum and maximum transaction amounts, and routes wrong items, cancellations and returns to the merchant ([Google Pay Help](https://support.google.com/googlepay/answer/16421147?hl=en-GB)). Meta's Muse shows an approval card with order details before checkout, pays through single-use Link cards tied to merchant, amount and period, and its leaked system prompt instructs it to ask one clarifying question at a time, never guess or carry sizes across brands, reopen every link before showing it, and build carts only from products flagged for agentic checkout in Meta's catalog ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/03/meta-muse-shopping-ai-agent.html); [The State of Brand](https://www.thestateofbrand.com/news/meta-muse-ai-shopping-agent-ranking-rules)). Claude Duet buys through a Stripe Link wallet that requires approval before spend ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/22/claude-duet-app-is-anthropics-bet-on-agents/)); Fo issues a fresh virtual card per purchase ([Carly](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/wajo-fo/)); Microsoft's Edge for Business agentic browsing preview executes each step "with the user's confirmation when required by policy" and consumer Edge was excluded ([Windows News](https://windowsnews.ai/article/edge-for-business-agentic-browsing-copilot-can-actunder-it-rules.419324)); Shopify's Fall '26 Sidekick plans multi-step changes and waits for merchant approval with an activity log attributing every change ([Shopify Editions](https://www.shopify.com/editions/fall2026)). On the payments side, Amex introduced an "Intent Intelligence" framework on 13 October to authenticate purchase intent before an agent transacts, with its April Agent Purchase Protection terms still unfinalized ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/13/amex-introduces-a-framework-to-prevent-ai-agents-from-making-unauthorized-purchases/)); Mastercard launched "Agentic Trust Intelligence" for banks and merchants to evaluate agent-initiated transactions ([AOL transcript](https://www.aol.com/articles/mastercard-ma-q3-2026-earnings-191318000.html)); and Chargebacks911 began selling merchants indemnification against qualifying chargebacks on AI-agent purchases on 26 October without disclosing pricing, caps or eligibility ([The Paypers](https://thepaypers.com/fraud-and-fincrime/news/chargebacks911-launches-indemnification-for-ai-agent-purchases)).

The stated reasons for these gates are worth recording because none of them is a measured error rate. Walmart's language is trust and control ("It's building trust," per Furner in August); Meta's Susan Li acknowledged on the Q3 call that some businesses are "understandably focused on protecting customer relationships" ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/21/meta-meta-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)); Visa's McInerney said "in agentic, trust is the biggest barrier to scale" ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/28/visa-v-q4-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)); Amazon's is unidentified agents; OpenAI's is unstated. The earlier trajectory report argued that confirmation would shrink to risk-routed exceptions while signed evidence persisted, and the window's designs match that shape: Walmart's cap-or-approve profile is risk routing by order value, Google's price ceiling is a deterministic rule, and Link's single-use card is an evidence artifact bound to merchant and amount. What the earlier report flagged as the kill condition for merchant-side rules, platforms enforcing caps upstream so the merchant never needs its own, has now arrived at Walmart; it has not arrived for the Shopify merchant auto-enrolled into Google, Microsoft and Meta channels, whose only controls are per-channel catalog and direct-checkout toggles with no global opt-out ([Shopify Community](https://community.shopify.com/t/why-cant-we-opt-out-of-agentic-storefronts/631140)). The one piece of detection infrastructure to ship broadly in the window was free: Microsoft Clarity's Agent Activity Dashboard went generally available on 29 October for any project with agentic traffic ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/checkout-appears-in-25-of-ai-agent-sessions-vs-15-for-humans-clarity-finds/)), which is the commoditization path the earlier report anticipated for standalone agent detection, though Forter's observation that merchants "cannot see who the real buyer is" behind an agent order and that agent orders show up as direct traffic or API calls remains true ([Forter](https://www.forter.com/blog/ai-agent-order-data/)).

## Conclusion

The production record of mid-2026 does not contain an accuracy problem; it contains an absence. Volume is small enough that even the parties who hold the data (two card networks, a dozen processors, eight fraud vendors) say the numbers are not yet meaningful, and the parties with an incentive to publish engagement (Meta, Amazon, Walmart, Shopify) stop precisely at the point where an order is placed. The one network that has opened its books shows the first real-world friction on agent orders was merchants' own fraud rules rejecting more than half of legitimate agent purchases, followed by cancellations for orders that should never have been eligible; those are rule and data failures a merchant can act on deterministically today, before any model error is visible. The team's product thesis survives this window, but its order of operations should follow the evidence: eligibility and geography rules and agent-order identification are needed now, because they address the failures that are actually occurring; signed intent and dispute evidence are needed before the dispute rate is known, because the liability regime is being written with the merchant as seller of record and consumers, merchants and networks all pointing at someone else; and universal human confirmation is the component the operators themselves are already building in, from Walmart's $50 caps to Muse's approval card, which makes it the least differentiated piece of the stack.

Two numbers will decide whether this is a business in 2027, and both arrive within weeks. If Forter's 12 November webinar shows agent-order disputes running materially above human orders, the evidence layer has a quantified loss line to price against; if Shopify's and Walmart's Q3 commentary still cannot distinguish agent-completed from AI-referred orders, the market for a merchant-side layer remains the handful of retailers large enough to build their own, and the long-tail merchant will keep seeing a few agent orders a month that look like direct traffic. Until then, every claim about agent-order accuracy in production, including the earlier report's 2–5% structural floor, is a projection, and the most defensible thing a founding team can say to a merchant is that nobody, including the agent operators, knows the rate.
